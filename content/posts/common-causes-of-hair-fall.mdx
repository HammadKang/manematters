Losing 50 to 100 hairs a day is completely normal. Hair grows in cycles, and the strands you see in the shower or on your brush are mostly hairs that have finished their life and made room for new ones. But if you notice much more hair than usual, a thinning part, or bald patches, something may be disrupting that cycle. Here are the seven most common causes of hair fall, and what you can do about each one.

## How the hair growth cycle works

Understanding the cycle makes everything else easier. Each hair goes through three stages. In the growth phase (anagen), which lasts two to six years, the hair actively grows. In the resting transition phase (catagen), growth slows for a couple of weeks. In the shedding phase (telogen), the hair rests for about three months and then falls out. Around 85 to 90 percent of your hairs are normally in the growth phase at any time.

Most causes of excess shedding work by pushing more hairs into the shedding phase at once. Because of the delay, you often notice the problem two to three months after the trigger.

## 1. Stress

High physical or emotional stress can push a large number of hairs into the shedding phase together. This is called telogen effluvium. A stressful period, such as exams, a bereavement, a job change or a long illness, can lead to heavy shedding a few months later.

**What to do:** The good news is that stress-related shedding is usually temporary. Hair typically recovers within six to nine months once the stress eases. Prioritise sleep, gentle exercise, and any relaxation routine that works for you.

## 2. Low iron, protein or other nutrients

Hair is made mostly of protein, and hair follicles are among the most active cells in the body, so they are sensitive to poor nutrition. Low iron, low ferritin, low protein, and deficiencies in vitamin D, zinc or B12 are all linked with increased shedding.

### Check your diet

Eggs, lentils, chickpeas, leafy greens, nuts, seeds, fish, yoghurt and lean meat all support healthy hair growth. Pair iron-rich foods with vitamin C, such as lemon or oranges, to help your body absorb the iron.

**What to do:** Do not start high-dose supplements on your own. Taking too much of some nutrients, such as vitamin A or selenium, can actually cause hair loss. Ask a doctor for a simple blood test to check your iron, ferritin, vitamin D and thyroid levels.

## 3. Hormonal changes

Pregnancy, childbirth, menopause, thyroid problems and conditions such as PCOS can all affect hair. After giving birth, many women notice heavy shedding around three months later, as hair that stayed in the growth phase during pregnancy moves into the shedding phase together. This usually settles within a year.

An underactive or overactive thyroid can cause diffuse thinning. PCOS can raise androgen levels and cause thinning at the crown along with other symptoms like irregular periods.

**What to do:** If your shedding comes with irregular periods, unusual tiredness, weight changes or very dry skin, see a doctor. Treating the underlying hormonal issue is the key to recovering your hair.

## 4. Harsh styling and heat

Everyday habits can quietly damage hair and cause breakage that looks like hair fall:

- Tight ponytails, buns and braids that pull on the roots, which can lead to traction alopecia
- Frequent bleaching, colouring or chemical straightening
- Daily high-heat tools like flat irons and curling irons
- Rough towel drying and brushing wet hair aggressively

**What to do:** Loosen your hairstyles, give your scalp breaks from tight styles, use a heat protectant, turn the temperature down, and detangle gently from the ends upward. Switching to a soft towel or an old cotton t-shirt for drying also helps.

## 5. Scalp problems

A healthy scalp is the foundation of healthy hair. Dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, fungal infections and psoriasis can inflame the scalp and weaken hair at the root. Constant scratching can also damage follicles.

**What to do:** Use a gentle anti-dandruff shampoo with ingredients such as zinc pyrithione or ketoconazole, and leave it on for a few minutes before rinsing. If you have thick scaling, painful bumps, or round patches of lost hair, see a dermatologist, since these need proper diagnosis.

## 6. Crash dieting, illness and medication

Sudden weight loss, very low-calorie diets, a high fever, major surgery or infections such as flu can all trigger temporary shedding. Some medications, including certain blood thinners, antidepressants, acne medicines and chemotherapy drugs, list hair loss as a side effect.

**What to do:** Aim for steady, balanced eating rather than extreme diets. If you suspect a medication is affecting your hair, never stop it on your own. Speak to your doctor, who may be able to suggest an alternative.

## 7. Genetics

Pattern hair loss, known as androgenetic alopecia, runs in families and is the most common cause of long-term thinning in both men and women. In men it often appears as a receding hairline and thinning at the crown. In women it usually shows as a widening part and overall thinner hair, with the hairline staying in place.

**What to do:** Genetic hair loss cannot be cured with home remedies, but it can be slowed. A doctor or dermatologist can discuss proven options and tell you which are suitable for you. Early advice gives the best results.

<Callout>See a doctor if hair loss is sudden, patchy or comes with scalp pain, itching, redness or other symptoms like fatigue and weight changes.</Callout>

## Simple everyday habits that help

Whatever the cause, a few habits support healthier hair:

1. Wash your hair regularly with a mild shampoo that suits your scalp type.
2. Be gentle when wet, because hair is most fragile when damp.
3. Eat enough protein and iron-rich foods.
4. Get seven to eight hours of sleep.
5. Avoid pulling your hair into tight styles every day.
6. Massage your scalp for a few minutes to relax and boost comfort.
7. Get a trim every two to three months to prevent split ends from travelling up the strand.

## When to get medical help

Normal shedding is usually evenly spread and does not leave visible thin areas. See a doctor if you lose clumps of hair, notice bald spots, see your scalp showing more clearly, or if shedding lasts longer than three to six months. A professional can examine your scalp, order tests and recommend the right treatment, which is far more reliable than guessing.

## Final thoughts

Hair fall is common and, in most cases, it is temporary and treatable. The key is to work out the cause instead of trying every product on the shelf. Start with the basics: nutrition, stress, gentle styling and a healthy scalp. If shedding continues, get a medical check so you can treat the real reason behind it.
